# LEGAL AUTHORITY MEMO — THOMPSON v. MATAGORDA COUNTY
## Case No. 3:25-cv-00253 | Objections to R&R (Dkt. 42)

*This memo compiles the relevant statutory text and case law supporting Plaintiff's objections to the Magistrate's Report and Recommendation.*

## PART I: TEXAS TRANSPORTATION CODE — RELEVANT STATUTORY TEXT

### § 502.407 — Expired Motor Vehicle Registration (Subtitle A)

**Text of § 502.407:**

> **(b)** If a person charged with an offense under this section provides to the court evidence that the person held valid registration for the vehicle on the date of the offense, the justice or judge shall dismiss the charge.
>
> **(b)(1)** The justice or judge may dismiss the charge if the defendant:
> > **(A)** remedies the defect not later than the 20th working day after the date of the offense or before the defendant's first court appearance date, whichever is later; **and**
> > **(B)** pays a dismissal fee of $20.
>
> **(b)(2)** If the defendant fails to comply with the dismissal requirements, the maximum penalty may not exceed a fine of $20.

**Significance:** The statute explicitly:
- Creates a dismissal right (not just discretion — the court "shall" dismiss upon proof of valid registration)
- Provides a 20-working-day cure period
- Caps the maximum penalty at $20
- Contains no provision for incarceration, criminal conviction, or criminal record

### § 543.003 — Arrest Procedures for Certain Vehicle Registration Offenses

**Text of § 543.003:**

> If a person is arrested for a violation of a vehicle registration offense and is not taken before a magistrate, the person shall be taken without unnecessary delay before the nearest magistrate in the county where the arrest occurs.

**Significance:**
- The phrase "without unnecessary delay" tracks federal constitutional requirements for presentment
- Critically: this statute governs **how** an arrest for certain registration offenses is to be conducted IF arrest occurs — it does not independently authorize arrest for expired registration
- The statute presupposes that arrest MAY occur for some registration offenses — but whether arrest is authorized for expired registration specifically is governed by the remedial scheme in § 502.407 (which is in Subtitle A)

### § 543.004 — Citation Procedures

**Text of § 543.004(c):**

> Notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter, if a person is alleged to have committed a violation of Section 545.413, 545.423, or 547.61 [speeding in school zone, wireless communication device, open container], the person is entitled to receive a written notice to appear not later than the 20th day after the date of the alleged violation.

**Critical Reading:** The phrase "notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter" is a **mandatory supersession clause** — it overrides other provisions to mandate citation for those three offenses. The **absence** of expired registration from this list does **not** mean arrest is permitted for expired registration. It means only that the mandatory-citation override does not apply.

**The R&R's error:** The Magistrate treated § 543.004(c) as an exhaustive list of offenses where citation is required. This is wrong. § 543.004(c) is a mandatory-citation provision for three specific offenses; it is not a ceiling on arrest restrictions.

### Classification Structure: Subtitle A vs. Subtitle B

The Texas Transportation Code is organized as follows:
- **Subtitle A: General Provisions** — **§ 502.407 (expired registration) is here** — administrative/regulatory offenses with specific remedial schemes (dismissal upon cure, $20 max, no incarceration)
- **Subtitle B: Rules of the Road** — criminal traffic offenses (DWI, reckless driving, speeding, etc.) — arrest authority under § 543.002 et seq.
- **Subtitle C: Vehicle Registration and Equipment** — separate category from expired registration; governs registration and equipment requirements with their own remedial schemes

Expired vehicle registration (§ 502.407) is in **Subtitle A** of the Texas Transportation Code, not Subtitle B. This is not a semantic distinction — it reflects the Texas Legislature's deliberate choice to classify registration offenses as administrative/regulatory matters subject to remedial schemes rather than criminal prosecutions.

## PART II: CASE LAW — ARREST AUTHORITY

### A. Atwater v. City of Lago Vista, 532 U.S. 318 (2001)

**Holding:** The Fourth Amendment does not forbid a warrantless arrest for a minor criminal offense, even when the offense is not punishable by imprisonment.

**Key language:** "If an officer has probable cause to believe that an individual has committed even a very minor criminal offense in his presence, he may, without violating the Fourth Amendment, arrest the offender." *Id.* at 354.

**Why it doesn't control here:**
1. *Atwater* addressed a seat belt violation under Texas law — this case does not turn on whether a Subtitle B offense was at issue.
2. The Texas Legislature has since evolved its statutory scheme — the placement of registration offenses in Subtitle A with a remedial scheme is post-*Atwater* legislative action.
3. *Atwater* addressed the **Fourth Amendment** question, not the **state statutory authority** question — specifically, *Virginia v. Moore* (553 U.S. 164, 2008) later clarified that the Fourth Amendment does not require probable cause to be measured by state law.
4. *Atwater* explicitly noted: "the business of the judiciary is to inquire into the sufficiency of the protestations of the officer." *Id.* at 354-55 n.17.

**Distinguishing feature:** *Atwater* involved an offense for which arrest was consistent with the statutory scheme. Here, § 502.407's remedial scheme is inconsistent with custodial arrest.

### B. Virginia v. Moore, 553 U.S. 164 (2008) — THE KEY ADVERSE PRECEDENT

**Holding:** Police may make warrantless arrests for offenses carrying only a fine, even where state law prohibited arrest. The Supreme Court held that "the Fourth Amendment does not require that probable cause be measured by state law."

**Why this is adverse:** If applied straightforwardly, *Moore* says that even if Texas law prohibits arrest for expired registration, the arrest does not violate the Fourth Amendment. This directly undercuts Plaintiff's Fourth Amendment theory.

**How to counter it:**
1. **Distinguish the nature of the state law at issue:** *Moore* involved a state law that merely prohibited arrest without providing any alternative remedy. Here, § 502.407 does not just prohibit arrest — it creates an **affirmative remedial framework** (dismissal upon compliance, $20 cap) that is categorically different from a simple prohibition.
2. **Focus on § 1983, not the Fourth Amendment:** The independent federal claim is that Deputy Gonzales violated Plaintiff's rights **under Texas law**, not under the Fourth Amendment. Section 1983 provides a cause of action for deprivation of constitutional and federal statutory rights by persons acting under color of state law. An unlawful arrest under Texas law is actionable regardless of whether the Fourth Amendment is violated.
3. **The "color of law" theory:** By enforcing an arrest procedure that exceeded his statutory authority, Deputy Gonzales acted under color of state law in a manner that deprived Plaintiff of rights secured by Texas law — which § 1983 protects.

### C. Cases Supporting the Proposition That Specific Statutes Limit Arrest Authority

**Jarecki v. G.D. Searle & Co., 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961):**
> "The principle is well established that where there is a conflict between a specific provision and a general provision, the specific provision prevails."

**RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank, 566 U.S. 639, 645 (2012):**
> "This rule reflects the 'common-sense canon of statutory construction' that 'a statute dealing with a narrow, specific topic' prevails over a statute dealing with a broader topic."

**Application here:** § 502.407 is a specific provision governing a narrow topic (expired registration). § 543.003 is a general provision governing arrest procedures. The specific provision (§ 502.407) prevails and limits the general provision (§ 543.003).

## PART III: QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Clearly Established Right Standard

**Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002):**
> "Qualified immunity is defeated if an official's conduct 'violates clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known.'"

**Mote v. Walthall, 902 F.3d 500, 505 (5th Cir. 2018):**
> "A right is clearly established only if the right's contours were sufficiently definite that any reasonable official in the defendant's shoes would have understood that he was violating it."

**Ashcroft v. al-Kidd, 563 U.S. 731, 741 (2011):**
> "We do not require a case directly on point, but existing precedent must have placed the statutory or constitutional question beyond debate."

### B. Why the Right Was Clearly Established Here

1. **The statutory text provided notice:** § 502.407 has existed in its current form for years. Its plain text establishes that expired registration is an administrative offense subject to dismissal upon compliance, with a maximum $20 penalty. A reasonable officer is charged with knowledge of the statutes he enforces.

2. **United States v. Lanier, 520 U.S. 259, 266-67 (1997):**
> "Officials can still be held liable if, on the facts of the case, no reasonable officer could have believed the action lawful."

3. **Thompson v. Upshur, 245 F.3d 447, 457 (5th Cir. 2001):**
Once a plaintiff demonstrates a constitutional violation, the defendant bears the burden to prove qualified immunity. The R&R never reached this burden-shifting analysis.

### C. The R&R's "Novelty" Argument Is Wrong

The R&R stated: "even if Thompson's theory is correct (it is not), his argument is so novel that Gonzales would be entitled to qualified immunity anyway."

**Why this fails:**
1. The legal principle — specific statutes limit general ones — has been established for centuries. The application to this specific factual scenario may be novel, but the principle itself is not.
2. The statutory text of § 502.407 provided Deputy Gonzales with fair warning that arrest was not the authorized remedy.
3. Qualified immunity analysis cannot simply assume the constitutional question is resolved against the plaintiff — that is circular reasoning.

## PART IV: DUE PROCESS

### A. Procedural Due Process — The Sham Magistration

**U.S. Const. amend. XIV:**
> "[N]or shall any State deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law."

**Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976):**
The Due Process Clause requires, at minimum: (1) notice of the charge; (2) an opportunity to be heard; and (3) a hearing before a neutral decisionmaker.

**Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950):**
> "An elementary and fundamental requirement of due process in any proceeding which is to be accorded finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections."

**Application:** Judge Sanders imposed "time served" — a criminal disposition — without:
- Filing a formal charge
- Accepting a plea
- Making a finding of guilt
- Advising Plaintiff of his right to cure under § 502.407(b)
- Giving Plaintiff the opportunity to invoke that right

This is precisely the "arbitrary governmental action" that due process prohibits.

### B. Brady v. Maryland — Suppression of Exculpatory Evidence

**Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963):**
> "The suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment."

**Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419, 433-34 (1995):**
Evidence is material if there is a "reasonable probability" that, had the evidence been disclosed, the result of the proceeding would have been different.

**Application:** Plaintiff specifically alleged that body cam and dash cam footage would have shown the vehicle was on **private property** (a friend's driveway) at the time of the stop — directly relevant to whether the tow was lawful. This footage was denied at the tow hearing and during public records requests. County Attorney Chau's suppression of this evidence at the tow hearing is actionable under Brady.

### C. County Attorney Chau — Not Entitled to Prosecutorial Immunity for Brady Violations

**Imbler v. Pachtman, 424 U.S. 409, 416 (1976):**
Prosecutorial immunity covers acts "as part of [the prosecutor's] traditional official functions."

**Distinction:** Chau's participation in the tow hearing — an administrative/administrative civil proceeding, not a criminal prosecution — was not a "traditional prosecutorial function." Moreover, Brady obligations exist independently of prosecutorial immunity. *See Youngblood v. West Virginia*, 547 U.S. 867, 869-70 (2006) (due process violated when police fail to preserve potentially useful evidence).

## PART V: MONELL LIABILITY

### A. Elements of Monell Claim

**Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658, 694 (1978):**
> "A municipality can be found liable under § 1983 on account of employee violations of constitutional rights only where the municipality's *official policy* or *custom* causes the deprivation."

**Piotrowski v. City of Houston, 237 F.3d 567, 578 (5th Cir. 2001):**
Three elements: (1) a **policymaker**; (2) an **official policy or custom**; and (3) a **violation of constitutional rights** whose "moving force" is the policy or custom.

### B. The "Moving Force" Argument

Even if individual officers are entitled to qualified immunity, the County's policy of:
- Failing to train officers on the Subtitle A (administrative registration) vs. Subtitle B (criminal traffic) distinction;
- Failing to advise arrestees of the § 502.407 cure right; and
- Failing to train JP court personnel on § 502.407 requirements

was the "moving force" behind the constitutional deprivation. Plaintiff did not merely encounter an individual bad actor — he encountered a **systemic failure** in how the County processes Subtitle A registration offenses.

### C. Discovery Is Essential

Plaintiff's Monell allegations are necessarily general because discovery has not been conducted. The County's training materials, internal communications, and prior complaints from similarly situated individuals are all discoverable. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, Plaintiff need not prove his Monell theory — he need only plead facts sufficient to state a plausible claim. *See Khai v. El Paso Indep. Sch. Dist.*, 445 F. Supp. 3d 9, 25 (W.D. Tex. 2020).

## PART VI: CIVIL CONSPIRACY — § 1985(3)

### A. Elements of § 1985(3) Claim

To state a civil conspiracy claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3), Plaintiff must show:
1. A **combination of two or more persons**;
2. An **unlawful object** to be accomplished;
3. A **meeting of the minds** on the object or course of action;
4. One or more **unlawful overt acts**; and
5. **Damages**.

*See Hill v. City of Pontotoc*, 983 F.3d 779, 788 (5th Cir. 2020).

### B. The Circumstantial Evidence

Plaintiff need not produce direct evidence of an explicit agreement. The Fifth Circuit has held that a conspiracy may be inferred from "circumstantial evidence of a 'meeting of the minds.'" *Id.*

Here, the coordinated sequence of events is highly probative:
1. Deputy Gonzales makes a custodial arrest for an offense the Legislature designated for non-custodial treatment.
2. Within 18 hours, Judge Sanders conducts a magistration that imposes "time served" — eliminating Plaintiff's § 502.407 cure right.
3. County Attorney Chau suppresses bodycam/dashcam footage at the tow hearing.
4. Records Clerk Riley denies public records requests despite Plaintiff's court-recognized indigency.
5. Judge Finlay rules the tow lawful despite lack of evidentiary foundation.

This sequential coordination — each step designed to prevent Plaintiff from obtaining relief — is the type of circumstantial evidence from which a jury could infer a conspiracy.

## PART VII: SUA SPONTE DISMISSAL — JACKSON TOWING

### A. Due Process Requires Notice and Opportunity to Amend

**Carroll v. Fort James Corp., 470 F.3d 1171, 1177 (5th Cir. 2006):**
> "As a general rule, a district court may dismiss a complaint on its own [under Rule 12(b)(6)] only after it has provided notice to the plaintiff and an opportunity to amend."

The R&R recommended sua sponte dismissal of Jackson Towing without issuing a show-cause order, without providing Plaintiff notice, and without granting leave to amend. This violated the procedural requirements of due process.

### B. Sufficient Facts Alleged Against Jackson

Accepting Plaintiff's allegations as true:
- Jackson Towing removed the vehicle from private property over the owner's objection.
- Jackson's representative testified at the tow hearing despite having no firsthand knowledge of the circumstances or vehicle location.
- Jackson participated in a hearing designed to ratify an unlawful tow.

These allegations, together with the pattern of coordinated conduct, state a plausible claim for civil conspiracy and conversion under Texas law.

## PART VIII: ROOKER-FELDMAN DOES NOT APPLY

**Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Indus. Corp., 544 U.S. 280, 284 (2005):**
Rooker-Feldman bars federal jurisdiction only over "cases brought by state-court losers complaining of injuries caused by state-court judgments rendered before the district court proceedings commenced and inviting district court review and rejection of those judgments."

**Dennis v. Spurs, 875 F.3d 865, 869 (5th Cir. 2017):**
Rooker-Feldman does not bar § 1983 claims that challenge defendants' conduct, not the state court judgment itself.

**Application:** Plaintiff's § 1983 claims challenge:
- The constitutionality of the initial arrest (independent of any state court proceeding)
- The constitutional adequacy of the post-arrest procedures
- The Brady violations at the tow hearing
- The conspiracy to conceal constitutional violations

These are independent federal claims. The R&R's reliance on Rooker-Feldman is error.
