The record before this Court consists of four documents: the Original Complaint (Dkt. 
1), the Defendants' Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 29), Plaintiff's Response in Opposition 
(Dkt. 33), and the Memorandum and Recommendation (Dkt. 42). A direct comparison 
of these documents reveals a single, dispositive defect: the Magistrate Judge did not 
evaluate the Complaint under the governing 12(b)(6) standard. Instead, he adopted 
the Defendants' contested legal conclusions verbatim, quoted their authority without 
acknowledging Plaintiff's distinctions, and resolved every disputed question of law 
and fact against the Plaintiff at the pleading stage.  
Each of the seven objections below identifies a specific manifestation of this 
structural failure.

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 72(b)(3) and 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1), the 
District Judge must determine de novo any part of the magistrate judge's disposition 
that has been properly objected to. A Rule 12(b)(6) review requires absolute judicial 
neutrality: the Court must accept all well-pleaded facts as true and draw all 
reasonable inferences in the Plaintiff's favor. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 
(2009). The Magistrate Judge abandoned this standard. As the objections below 
demonstrate, he adopted the Defendants' Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 29) as the 
framework for his analysis and ignored Plaintiff's explicit rebuttals (Dkt. 33).  
II. OBJECTIONS  
Objection 1: The Magistrate Judge Adopted Defendants' Retroactive 
Justification for the Seizure, Ignoring Plaintiff's Pleaded Facts.  
Plaintiff explicitly pleaded that the seizure and detention were "conducted 
without probable cause" (Dkt. 1, ¶39). At the 12(b)(6) stage, the Magistrate was 
required to accept this fact as true. Instead, he adopted the Defendants' logical 
fallacy: that the arrest was lawful simply because the registration was expired (Dkt. 
29, p. 10). Plaintiff warned the Court that Defendants were "assuming the conclusion 
they are required to prove" (Dkt. 33, p. 8). The Magistrate nonetheless ruled: "The 
central dispute in this case is whether it was lawful for Gonzales to arrest Thompson 
for an expired registration. Thompson concedes in his complaint that his registration 
was expired." (Dkt. 42, p. 3).  


=== PAGE 3 ===
This is a failure of the 12(b)(6) standard and a violation of the Fourth 
Amendment. Under well-established Fourth Amendment law, the legality of a seizure 
must be justified at its inception; an officer may not initiate a stop based on facts that 
become known only after the stop has occurred, and probable cause must exist at the 
time of the seizure, not be reconstructed after the fact. By relying on Plaintiff's 
post-incident admission to cure the  
Case 3:25-cv-00253 Document 43 Filed 05/05/26 in TXSD Page 3 of 10 
officer's lack of probable cause prior to the stop, the Magistrate permitted 
unconstitutional retroactive justification.  
Moreover, the Magistrate conflated two distinct Fourth Amendment inquiries: 
(1) whether probable cause existed to initiate the stop, and (2) whether probable 
cause existed to effect a custodial arrest. The existence of an expired registration 
does not establish that Deputy Gonzales observed it before activating his lights, nor 
does it establish that a custodial arrest was authorized under Texas law for a 
non-jailable Class C misdemeanor. These are separate constitutional questions that 
the M&R impermissibly collapsed into a single, defense-favorable conclusion.  
Objection 2: The Magistrate Judge Ignored Plaintiff's Fourteenth 
Amendment Due Process Claim by Analyzing § 502.407 Solely Through 
the Defendants' Fourth Amendment Lens.  
The Magistrate (Dkt. 42, p. 4) analyzed Texas Transportation Code § 502.407 
solely to determine whether an officer can arrest for expired registration, mirroring the 
Defendants' argument. He completely ignored Plaintiff's Fourteenth Amendment Due 
Process claim (Dkt. 1, ¶22; Dkt. 33, pp. 4-6). Section 502.407(b) grants a citizen the 
statutory right to remedy an expired registration within 20 working days, resulting in 
dismissal. Plaintiff pleaded he was denied this opportunity and immediately given a 


=== PAGE 4 ===
criminal disposition of "time served" (Dkt. 1, ¶21-22).  
The statutory right created by § 502.407(b)—the right to remedy the defect 
within 20 working days and receive a dismissal—is a state-created liberty and 
property interest protected by the Due Process Clause. It is well-established that 
statutory entitlements create property interests protected by due process. Judge 
Sanders's imposition of "time served" affirmatively extinguished this statutory right. 
The arbitrary denial of a state-created statutory right violates the Due Process 
Clause.

The Magistrate characterized Plaintiff's § 502.407 argument as merely 
"creative" (Dkt. 42, p. 4) rather than addressing it on its legal merits. A plaintiff's 
reliance on the plain text of a statute is not "creative"—it is the most traditional form of 
legal argument. The Legislature enacted § 502.407's remedial framework for the 
precise situation at issue in this case. Dismissing the argument without legal analysis 
is error.  
Objection 3: The Magistrate Judge Ignored Pleaded Facts to Grant Absolute 
Immunity for Acts Taken in the Clear Absence of Jurisdiction.  
The Magistrate (Dkt. 42, p. 5) concluded Judge Sanders "acted well within his 
jurisdiction when he credited Thompson's time served" under Texas Code of Criminal 
Procedure Art. 45A.251(d). This ruling violates the 12(b)(6) standard because the 
statute, by its own terms, requires a prerequisite that Plaintiff alleges did not occur. 
Article 45A.251(d) provides that a justice of the peace "may assess a fine" after a 
defendant "enters a plea" or is found guilty. Tex. Code Crim. Proc. Art. 45A.251(d). 
Plaintiff explicitly pleaded that Judge Sanders entered this disposition "without a plea, 
and without advising Plaintiff of his rights" (Dkt. 1, ¶21). The Magistrate was required 


=== PAGE 5 ===
to accept as true that no plea occurred. Instead, he applied a statute that, on its own 
terms, requires a prerequisite that Plaintiff alleges did not occur—and resolved that 
factual dispute in the Defense's favor.  
A judge who unilaterally imposes a criminal sentence on an unadjudicated 
citizen acts in the "clear absence of all jurisdiction" and is stripped of absolute 
immunity. Stump v. Sparkman, 435 U.S. 349, 356-57 (1978). The Magistrate 
improperly resolved this factual dispute in favor of the Defense at the pleading stage.  
Objection 4: The Magistrate Judge Rewrote Plaintiff's Complaint to Grant 
Qualified Immunity.  
Rather than accepting Plaintiff's facts as true, the Magistrate sanitized the 
Defendants' conduct, stating Chau was "simply participating in a tow hearing" and 
Riley was "simply

refusing to waive fees" (Dkt. 42, p. 6). Plaintiff did not plead they "simply participated." 
Plaintiff pleaded they knowingly withheld material evidence and intentionally created 
administrative barriers to cover up an unlawful arrest (Dkt. 1, ¶28-29, 46-50).  
At the 12(b)(6) stage, a court may not rewrite the complaint to minimize 
alleged constitutional violations. A court evaluating a motion to dismiss does not 
weigh the evidence or make credibility determinations, and may not recast the 
plaintiff's allegations to minimize their constitutional significance. By recharacterizing 
deliberate suppression as mere participation, the Magistrate engaged in precisely the 
recasting that the 12(b)(6) standard forbids.

Objection 7: The Magistrate Judge Erred by Completely Ignoring the Claims 
Against the Unidentified "John Doe" and "Jane Doe" Deputies.  
Plaintiff named "JOHN DOE DEPUTY, Transport Deputy" and "JANE DOE 
DEPUTY, On-Scene Supervisor" as defendants in their individual and official 
capacities (Dkt. 1, ¶12-13), pleading that multiple deputies participated in the unlawful 
seizure, the transportation, and the subsequent search of Plaintiff's vehicle. The

Defendants' Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 29) failed to address their specific conduct. 
Consequently, the M&R (Dkt. 42) omits any 12(b)(6) analysis regarding their actions.  
The use of Doe defendants in civil rights actions is well-established and 
permits the plaintiff, through discovery, to identify the specific individuals whose 
conduct violated his constitutional rights. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(c) (relation back of

analysis or opportunity for discovery to ascertain their identities is reversible error.

IV. THE STRUCTURAL PATTERN  
Three features of the M&R confirm that the Magistrate Judge did not independently 
evaluate the legal questions presented.  
A. Citation Pattern. The M&R cites Dkt. 29 repeatedly and favorably. It cites 
Dkt. 33 only twice: once to dismiss Plaintiff's § 502.407 argument as "creative," and 
once to mischaracterize Plaintiff's qualified-immunity argument as a failure to contest 
immunity. Dkt. 42 at 4, 6. It never cites Dkt. 33 for the purpose of engaging with its 
arguments. This pattern reveals that the M&R treated the Defense motion as its 
primary source and Plaintiff's opposition as basically nonexistent or just an 
afterthought.  
B. Logical Structure. The Defense motion follows a single logical chain: (1) 
the arrest was lawful; (2) therefore all officials are immune; (3) therefore no 
underlying violation exists;  
Case 3:25-cv-00253 Document 43 Filed 05/05/26 in TXSD Page 8 of 10 
(4) therefore all derivative claims fail. The M&R follows this exact chain. It does not 


=== PAGE 9 ===
independently evaluate each claim; it applies the Defense's logical structure and 
reaches the Defense's conclusions. This is adoption, not adjudication.  
C. Standard Applied. On a motion to dismiss, the Court must accept the 
plaintiff's factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the 
plaintiff's favor. Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. The M&R states this standard but does not 
apply it. It accepts the Defense's legal conclusions as true (the arrest was lawful; 
probable cause existed; immunity applies) and draws all inferences in the Defense's 
favor. The Complaint alleges that the stop and arrest were without probable cause, 
that due process was violated, and that evidence was suppressed. Dkt. 1 at 6-10. 
These allegations must be accepted as true at this stage. The M&R assumes they 
are false and builds its analysis on that assumption.  
V. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF  
The Magistrate Judge characterizes Plaintiff's reliance on the plain text of the law as 
"creativity" (Dkt. 42, p. 4).  
But it requires impermissible judicial "creativity" to retroactively invent probable 
cause, to recharacterize the deliberate suppression of evidence as an innocent glitch, 
and to grant absolute immunity for a criminal sentence imposed without a plea. The 
M&R is not a neutral 12(b)(6) evaluation; it is a verbatim adoption of the Defendants' 
flawed Motion to Dismiss.
