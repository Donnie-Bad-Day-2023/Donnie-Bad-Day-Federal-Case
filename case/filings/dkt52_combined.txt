II. LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), a claim may be dismissed because of a "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." A complaint must contain a "short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief" to give the defendant "fair notice" of what the claims are and the grounds upon which they rest. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2); Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). "A plaintiff's obligation to provide the 'grounds' of his 'entitlement to relief' requires more than labels and conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do . . .." Id. at 555 (internal citations omitted). Legal conclusions "must be supported by factual allegations." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 679 (2009). To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual allegations, accepted as true, "to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." Id. at 678 (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). "Where a complaint pleads facts that are 'merely consistent with' a defendant's liability, it 'stops short of the line between possibility and plausibility of entitlement to relief.'" Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557). Conclusory allegations shall not be accepted as true. Kaiser Aluminum & Chem. Sales, Inc. v. Avondale Shipyards, Inc., 677 F.2d 1045, 1050 (5th Cir. 1982).

III. ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITIES

A. Plaintiff's alleged facts do not support the notion that the stop was conducted without probable cause.

When ruling on the Matagorda County Defendants' original Motion to Dismiss, this Court held that "[t]o survive another motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must allege facts that, when accepted as true, support the notion that the stop was conducted without probable cause." Order Adopting Magistrate Judge's Mem. & Recommendation, Dkt. No. 44, at 2. Plaintiff's Amended Complaint falls short.

As explained by Judge Edison, an officer may arrest an individual if the officer has probable cause to believe that the individual committed even a minor criminal offense in his presence. Mem. & Recommendation, Dkt. No. 42, at 3. Plaintiff argues that it was a "physical, optical, and geometric impossibility for Defendant Gonzales to visually observe the status of Plaintiff's driver's-side registration sticker" based on the parties' locations and the fact that the stop occurred at night. Pl.'s Am. Compl., Dkt. No. 45 ¶ 23. However, Plaintiff's own factual allegations contradict this contention. Plaintiff claims that he was stopped at a 4-way intersection, traveling west. Id. ¶ 22. He then states that Officer Gonzales was traveling north. Id. ¶ 23. [a]

Plaintiff argues that this "plac[ed Officer Gonzales's] vehicle at a perpendicular, strict passenger-side orientation relative to Plaintiff's vehicle"; thus, Plaintiff contends, it was impossible for the officer to see Plaintiff's registration sticker on the driver's side of the windshield. Id. Putting aside any arguments regarding whether drivers at four-way traffic stops can only observe the nearest side of a car, Plaintiff's own asserted orientations would place Officer Gonzales as perpendicular to the driver's side of Plaintiff's vehicle, where Plaintiff's expired registration sticker was placed. Additionally, Plaintiff contends that he "executed a lawful left-hand turn to travel southbound." Pl.'s Am. Compl., Dkt. No. 45 ¶ 24. Again taking Plaintiff's allegations as true, when Plaintiff turned left to travel south, he would have directly faced Officer Gonzales, who was traveling north. [j] XIV. DEFENDANTS' CONCESSION THAT THE OFFICER "DID NOT RUN THE PLATE" CONFIRMS A TOTAL LACK OF PRE-SEIZURE PROBABLE CAUSE AS A MATTER OF LAW

In an extraordinary misapplication of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence, Defendants explicitly argue that "Officer Gonzales was not required to run Plaintiff's license plate because he observed the expired registration status from the sticker" and seek to use Plaintiff's subsequent 2026 pleading admissions to validate a 2023 seizure (Dkt. 52, p. 4).

First, it is an unyielding tenet of constitutional law that the legality of a seizure must be evaluated solely based on the objective facts known to the officer at the exact moment the seizure is initiated. An admission made by a civil plaintiff years after the fact cannot travel backward through time to retroactively provide an officer with a legal foundation at 22:20 on the night of the stop.

Second, by explicitly conceding that Officer Gonzales did not run Plaintiff's license plate prior to or during the stop (Dkt. 52, p. 4), Defendants have effectively confessed to a severe Fourth Amendment violation.

An unverified visual observation of a windshield sticker is not a confirmed criminal offense until it is electronically or physically verified.

[k] Gonzales "was not required to run the plate," Defendants admit that the officer escalated a brief, unverified Class C misdemeanor traffic stop into a full, prolonged custodial over-detention and vehicle seizure without ever verifying the legal status of the vehicle. A bare, unverified visual hunch can never serve as a legal substitute for the mandatory verification required to strip a citizen of their liberty.

Plaintiff also claims that Officer Gonzales's incident report misidentifies Plaintiff's vehicle as a pickup. Id. ¶ 27. A simple misclassification does not negate the existence of probable cause in stopping Plaintiff's vehicle. [l]
