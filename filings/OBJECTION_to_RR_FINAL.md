## I. LEGAL STANDARD

When a party files timely written objections to a magistrate judge's recommendation, the district court must conduct **de novo review** of "any portion of the magistrate judge's disposition that has been properly objected to." 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1)(C); Fed. R. Civ. P. 72(b)(3). The district court may adopt or reject the recommendation, but it "may not defer to the magistrate judge's recommendation" when valid objections have been raised. *Perrier v. FDIC*, 506 F.3d 444, 451 (5th Cir. 2007). The de novo review extends to all matters contained in objections, including those bearing on legal conclusions and factual findings. *G其中 a v. Stephens*, 465 F.3d 198, 203-04 (5th Cir. 2006).

## II. OBJECTION NO. 1: THE R&R'S STATUTORY ANALYSIS IS LEGALLY ERRONEOUS — § 543.004(c) IS NOT AN EXHAUSTIVE LIST

### A. The R&R's Central Statutory Error

The R&R's foundational statutory holding is:

> "Thompson overlooks that, under Texas law, speeding, use of a wireless communication device, or a violation of the open container law 'are the only offenses for which issuance of a written notice to appear is mandatory.' Tex. Transp. Code § 543.004(c) (emphasis added). Whatever prohibition against arrest Thompson believes is implied by § 502.407 cannot stand in the face of the plain language of § 543.004, which articulates exactly three offenses for which officers may not make an arrest, none of which is an expired registration."

**This is a fundamental misreading of § 543.004(c).** The statute does not say: "these three and only these three offenses require citation." It says:

> "Notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter, if a person is alleged to have committed a violation of Section 545.413, 545.423, or 547.61, the person is entitled to receive a written notice to appear not later than the 20th day after the date of the alleged violation." Tex. Transp. Code § 543.004(c).

The phrase "notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter" is a **mandatory supersession clause** applicable only to those three specific offenses. It means: *even if other provisions of the Transportation Code would authorize different treatment, these three offenses require mandatory citation regardless of officer discretion.* It does **not** mean: "because these three are the only ones with mandatory citation, all other offenses permit arrest at officer discretion."

The logical gap in the R&R's reasoning is critical: **the absence of an offense from § 543.004(c)'s list does not establish officer discretion to arrest.** It merely means the mandatory-citation override does not apply to that offense. Whether arrest is otherwise permitted depends on whether other statutes — such as § 502.407's specific remedial framework — independently limit that discretion.

### B. § 502.407 Creates a Specific Remedial Scheme That Limits Arrest Authority

Chapter 502 of the Texas Transportation Code places expired vehicle registration in **Subtitle A** — "General Provisions." This is categorically distinct from Subtitle B — "Rules of the Road" — which governs criminal traffic offenses (DWI, reckless driving, speeding, etc.). The Legislature's deliberate choice to place registration offenses in Subtitle A with a specific remedial dismissal scheme reflects an express legislative classification: these are **administrative/regulatory offenses**, not criminal traffic offenses.

The remedial scheme under § 502.407 is fundamentally incompatible with custodial arrest:

- A person charged with expired registration may obtain **dismissal** by remedying the defect within **20 working days** or before the first court appearance, whichever is later. Tex. Transp. Code § 502.407(b)(1)(A).
- The maximum consequence upon failure to comply is a **$20 administrative fee**. *Id.* § 502.407(b)(2).
- There is **no provision for incarceration**, no **criminal finding of guilt**, and no **criminal record** upon compliance.
- The court **shall dismiss** the charge upon proof of valid registration — this is mandatory, not discretionary. *Id.* § 502.407(a).

This is not a minor regulatory detail. The statutory scheme explicitly contemplates a non-custodial, non-punitive resolution. When the Legislature says an offense is subject to dismissal upon compliance, carries a maximum $20 penalty, and is classified in Subtitle A of the General Provisions with a remedial dismissal scheme, the necessary implication is that **custodial arrest is not the intended enforcement mechanism.**

This is an application of the canon of statutory construction: **lex specialis derogat legi generali** — when a specific statute governs a particular subject, it controls over a general statute. *Jarecki v. G.D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961); *RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank*, 566 U.S. 639, 645 (2012) ("a statute dealing with a narrow, specific topic prevails over a statute dealing with a broader topic"). The specific remedial framework of § 502.407 operates as a limitation on the general arrest authority that might otherwise exist under § 543.003.

### C. The Atwater Analysis Is Incomplete

*Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001), is distinguishable and does not control this case. In *Atwater*, the Supreme Court addressed whether arrest for a seatbelt violation violated the **Fourth Amendment** — and held it did not, given the arrest was supported by probable cause and the offense was criminal in nature. Critically, *Atwater* addressed a **Subtitle B** offense — a criminal traffic misdemeanor.

The Texas Transportation Code's structural organization — with Subtitle A establishing a specific remedial framework for registration offenses — represents post-*Atwater* legislative evolution that narrows the class of offenses for which arrest is appropriate. A custodial arrest for an administrative offense governed by § 502.407's remedial scheme in Subtitle A is a distinct category from the *Atwater* scenario, and the R&R's failure to acknowledge this structural distinction renders its *Atwater* analysis legally insufficient.

**Requested Relief:** The Court should conduct de novo review of whether § 502.407's remedial framework in Subtitle A operates as a limitation on arrest authority for expired registration, and whether Deputy Gonzales's arrest violated Texas law.

## III. OBJECTION NO. 2: VIRGINIA V. MOORE IS CONTROLLING AND MUST BE DISTINGUISHED — BUT PLAINTIFF'S § 1983 CLAIM IS INDEPENDENT OF THE FOURTH AMENDMENT

### A. The R&R's Failure to Address Virginia v. Moore Is Reversible Error

The R&R's constitutional analysis relies exclusively on *Atwater*. It does not mention, let alone analyze, *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008). This omission is legally significant because *Moore* is the controlling Supreme Court precedent on the precise question the R&R addresses: whether an arrest that violates state law gives rise to a Fourth Amendment violation.

In *Moore*, the Supreme Court held: "We have not previously held that the Fourth Amendment is violated when police violate state law in making an arrest supported by probable cause." The Court further stated: "The Fourth Amendment does not require that probable cause be measured by state law."

This is adverse to Plaintiff's Fourth Amendment theory in one sense. If the Court applies *Moore* straightforwardly, it does not matter whether Texas law prohibits arrest for expired registration — the Fourth Amendment still would not be violated. The defense will raise this, and the Court will expect Plaintiff to have addressed it.

### B. Distinguishing Moore: The Affirmative Statutory Remedial Scheme

However, *Moore* is distinguishable on a critical basis. In *Moore*, Virginia law **prohibited** arrest but provided **no alternative remedial scheme** — it was simply a restriction on arrest authority. Here, Texas law does not merely prohibit arrest for expired registration — it provides an **affirmative remedial framework** categorically incompatible with arrest:

- § 502.407 creates a right to **mandatory dismissal** upon compliance;
- The maximum penalty is **$20** — not incarceration, not a criminal fine;
- The remedy is administrative, not criminal;
- The statute is located in **Subtitle A** (General Provisions), expressly placing registration offenses in an administrative classification separate from criminal traffic enforcement.

A reasonable officer reading § 502.407 would not simply understand that arrest is "prohibited" — he would understand that arrest is **inconsistent with the entire remedial scheme the Legislature established** for this category of offense. The *Moore* Court specifically distinguished cases where "the state statute at issue did not simply prohibit arrest, but provided an entirely different regulatory approach." 553 U.S. at 175-76. This is precisely that situation.

### C. The § 1983 Claim Is Independent of the Fourth Amendment

Even if the Court determines that the arrest did not violate the Fourth Amendment under *Moore* and *Atwater*, Plaintiff has an **independent federal claim under § 1983** for deprivation of rights under color of state law. Section 1983 provides: "Every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State ... subjects, or causes to be subjected, any citizen ... to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured."

An unlawful arrest under Texas law — one that exceeds the arresting officer's statutory authority under the Subtitle A remedial scheme — is actionable under § 1983 as a deprivation of the right to be free from arrest that violates state law, **regardless of whether the Fourth Amendment is violated**. The constitutional hook is the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment: the right to be free from state action that exceeds lawful authority.

**Requested Relief:** The Court should (1) conduct de novo review of whether *Virginia v. Moore* applies and whether the specific remedial scheme of § 502.407 distinguishes this case; (2) confirm that Plaintiff's § 1983 claim for unlawful arrest under Texas law is independent of the Fourth Amendment analysis; and (3) reject the R&R's recommendation that Count I be dismissed on qualified immunity grounds.

## IV. OBJECTION NO. 3: THE R&R'S QUALIFIED IMMUNITY ANALYSIS IS CIRCULAR

### A. The R&R Assumed Its Central Conclusion

The R&R states: "even if Thompson's theory is correct (it is not), his argument is so novel that Gonzales would be entitled to qualified immunity anyway." Dkt. 42 at 5.

This reasoning is circular and legally erroneous.

**First**, qualified immunity protects officers only when the constitutional right at issue was not "clearly established." The question is not whether Thompson's specific theory had been previously litigated — it is whether the **constitutional right's contours** were sufficiently definite that a reasonable officer would understand he was violating it. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The right at issue is not a newly invented constitutional doctrine — it is the principle that an officer may not arrest for an offense that the Texas Legislature has placed in Subtitle C with a non-custodial remedial scheme and a $20 maximum penalty. A reasonable officer reading § 502.407 would understand that custodial arrest is not the intended enforcement mechanism.

**Second**, the R&R conflates two distinct questions: (1) whether a constitutional right was clearly established, and (2) whether the officer's conduct was objectively unreasonable. Once a plaintiff demonstrates a constitutional violation, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove qualified immunity. *See Thompson v. Upshur*, 245 F.3d 447, 457 (5th Cir. 2001). The R&R never reaches this burden-shifting inquiry because it assumes the conclusion that no constitutional violation occurred.

**Third**, the R&R's reliance on *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731 (2011), is misplaced. *al-Kidd* involved a dramatic expansion of executive power — the use of a federal material witness warrant as a pretextual pre-trial detention. This case involves the **narrowing** of arrest authority based on a specific statutory remedial scheme. These are opposite ends of the constitutional spectrum.

### B. The Plain Text of § 502.407 Provided Clear Notice

Critically, § 502.407 has existed in its current form for years. Its text is unambiguous: expired registration is an administrative offense with a specific non-custodial remedy. Officers are charged with knowledge of the statutes they enforce. *See United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 266-67 (1997) (holding that "fair warning" can be provided by the plain text of a statute even without case law). The statute's express provision for dismissal upon compliance and its $20 maximum penalty are clear indicators that custodial arrest was not the intended enforcement mechanism.

**Requested Relief:** The Court should conduct de novo review of the qualified immunity analysis, apply the correct burden-shifting framework, and find that the right to be free from custodial arrest for an administrative offense governed by the Subtitle A remedial scheme of § 502.407 was clearly established by the plain text of the statute.

## V. OBJECTION NO. 4: THE R&R FAILED TO ADDRESS PLAINTIFF'S PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS CLAIMS

### A. The Sham Magistration Violated Due Process

Plaintiff's Opposition (Dkt. 33) raised a specific due process claim: Judge Sanders entered a "time served" disposition — a criminal punitive disposition — without:

1. A formal charge being filed;
2. A plea being offered or accepted;
3. A finding of guilt being made;
4. Plaintiff being advised of his right to invoke the § 502.407(b) cure remedy;
5. Plaintiff being given the opportunity to cure the defect within the statutory 20-working-day period.

The R&R addresses judicial immunity but does not address the **procedural adequacy** of the magistration itself. This is a distinct due process question: even if Judge Sanders is immune from damages, was the procedure used constitutionally deficient?

The Due Process Clause requires, at minimum, that a person be afforded notice of the charge and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before a punitive disposition is entered. *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976); *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). A "time served" disposition entered without a charge, without a plea, without an adjudication, and without notice of the statutory cure right is precisely the kind of arbitrary governmental action the Due Process Clause prohibits.

### B. Brady — Suppression of Exculpatory Evidence

Plaintiff specifically alleged that body camera and dash camera footage would have shown the vehicle was on **private property** (a friend's driveway) at the time of the stop — directly relevant to whether the tow was lawful and whether Deputy Gonzales had probable cause to believe the registration was expired in the manner charged. County Attorney Chau suppressed this footage at the tow hearing. Records Clerk Riley denied Plaintiff's public records requests despite his court-recognized indigency.

Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963), and *Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419, 433-34 (1995), the suppression of materially exculpatory evidence by the prosecution violates due process. The R&R does not address this claim.

County Attorney Chau is **not entitled to prosecutorial immunity** for her conduct at the tow hearing. Prosecutorial immunity covers acts "as part of [the prosecutor's] traditional official functions." *Imbler v. Pachtman*, 424 U.S. 409, 416 (1976). A tow hearing is an **administrative civil proceeding**, not a criminal prosecution. Chau's participation in blocking evidence at that hearing was not a "traditional prosecutorial function" and falls outside the scope of absolute immunity.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of the procedural due process claim, distinguish between judicial immunity (which bars damages against Judge Sanders personally) and the merits of the due process claim (which survives against the County under Monell), and find that Plaintiff has stated a plausible due process claim.

## VII. OBJECTION NO. 6: ROOKER-FELDMAN DOES NOT APPLY

The R&R invokes Rooker-Feldman in a footnote to bar Plaintiff's challenges to the state court proceedings. This is error.

The Rooker-Feldman doctrine bars federal district courts from exercising jurisdiction over "cases brought by state-court losers complaining of injuries caused by state-court judgments rendered before the district court proceedings commenced and inviting district court review and rejection of those judgments." *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Indus. Corp.*, 544 U.S. 280, 284 (2005).

Plaintiff is not asking this Court to review or reverse any state court judgment. Plaintiff's claims are independent federal claims under § 1983 that:

1. The **initial arrest** was unlawful under Texas law;
2. The subsequent **magistration and records denial** violated federal due process; and
3. Defendants **conspired** to conceal constitutional violations.

These claims do not require the Court to "review and reject" any state court judgment. They are claims about ongoing constitutional violations by government officials — precisely the claims § 1983 was designed to address.

The Supreme Court has expressly preserved the distinction between direct appellate review of state court judgments and independent federal claims: "A § 1983 action is a 'parallel' suit, not an appeal, when the plaintiff is not asking the district court to review and reverse the state court judgment, but to redress injuries caused by the state actors' unconstitutional conduct." *Argo v. Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Md.*, 339 F.3d 1089, 1096 (9th Cir. 2003); *see also Dennis v. Spurs*, 875 F.3d 865, 869 (5th Cir. 2017) (Rooker-Feldman does not bar § 1983 claims that challenge defendants' conduct, not the state court judgment itself).

**Requested Relief:** The Court should reject the Rooker-Feldman argument and confirm that Plaintiff's claims are independent federal claims under § 1983.

## VIII. OBJECTION NO. 7: THE R&R'S SUA SPONTE DISMISSAL OF JACKSON TOWING VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

### A. No Notice, No Opportunity to Be Heard

The R&R recommends sua sponte dismissing Plaintiff's claims against Jackson Towing Services "before the court enters final judgment" based solely on the observation that "there are only two references to Jackson in the complaint." Dkt. 42 at 7-8.

This dismissal violates due process for three reasons:

1. **No motion was filed regarding Jackson**: The defense's Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 29) addressed only the Matagorda County Defendants. Jackson was not mentioned.
2. **No sua sponte show-cause order was issued**: The Court did not order Plaintiff to show cause why Jackson should not be dismissed.
3. **No opportunity to amend**: Plaintiff was not given notice that sua sponte dismissal was being considered and had no opportunity to file an amended complaint with additional factual allegations.

A sua sponte dismissal without prior notice and opportunity to amend violates the Due Process Clause. *See Carroll v. Fort James Corp.*, 470 F.3d 1171, 1177 (5th Cir. 2006) ("As a general rule, a district court may dismiss a complaint on its own [under Rule 12(b)(6)] only after it has provided notice to the plaintiff and an opportunity to amend."); *Alert One Co. v. Hydro-Aid, Inc.*, 299 F.3d 746, 748 (5th Cir. 2002).

### B. Plaintiff Has Stated a Plausible Claim Against Jackson

Accepting the complaint's allegations as true: Jackson Towing removed Plaintiff's vehicle from private property over the owner's objection; County Attorney Chau "permitted testimony from a Jackson Towing representative who lacked any firsthand knowledge of the towing circumstances or vehicle location"; and Jackson participated in a hearing designed to ratify an unlawful tow. These allegations are sufficient to state a plausible claim for civil conspiracy and conversion under Texas law.

**Requested Relief:** Any dismissal of Jackson Towing should be **with leave to amend**, after appropriate notice, so that Plaintiff may allege additional factual detail.

## IX. OBJECTION NO. 8: THE R&R FAILED TO ADDRESS COUNT IV — CIVIL CONSPIRACY UNDER § 1985(3)

### A. The R&R Is Silent on Count IV

Plaintiff's Complaint and Opposition raised specific allegations of civil conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3): coordinated actions among Deputy Gonzales, Judge Sanders, County Attorney Chau, Records Clerk Riley, and Judge Finlay to (1) arrest Plaintiff for a non-arrestable offense; (2) eliminate Plaintiff's § 502.407 remedy through a baseless magistration; (3) conceal the circumstances of the arrest by denying footage; and (4) ratify the unlawful tow at a procedurally defective hearing.

The R&R does not contain a separate analysis of Count IV. It does not address the specific coordinated actions alleged, the overt acts, or the legal standard for § 1985(3) conspiracy liability.

### B. The Coordination Is Sufficiently Pled

The Fifth Circuit has held that a civil conspiracy under § 1985(3) may be established by circumstantial evidence of a "meeting of the minds." *Hill v. City of Pontotoc*, 983 F.3d 779, 788 (5th Cir. 2020). The sequential, coordinated pattern here — arrest for a non-arrestable offense, immediate magistration eliminating the cure right, denial of footage, and a one-sided tow hearing — is precisely the type of circumstantial evidence from which a conspiracy may be inferred.

The R&R notes that County Attorney Chau "is entitled to prosecutorial immunity" for her conduct. But prosecutorial immunity is an affirmative defense — it does not defeat the conspiracy claim at the pleading stage. Moreover, Chau's conduct at the tow hearing was not a "traditional prosecutorial function" and immunity does not attach to civil conspiracy claims under § 1985(3). *See Heffernan v. Hunter*, 189 F.3d 405, 413 (3d Cir. 1999) (prosecutorial immunity does not extend to participation in a conspiracy under § 1985(3) when the prosecutor acted outside the scope of traditional prosecutorial functions).

**Requested Relief:** The Court should conduct de novo review of Count IV and address whether Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to state a plausible civil conspiracy claim.
