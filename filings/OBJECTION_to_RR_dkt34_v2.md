## I. INTRODUCTION

The Magistrate's Report and Recommendation ("R&R") warrants rejection for three fundamental reasons.

**First**, the R&R committed a reversible error of statutory interpretation: it treated Texas Transportation Code § 543.004(c)'s list of three offenses requiring mandatory citation as an exhaustive enumeration of all offenses where citation is required — thereby implying arrest is permitted for every unlisted offense. This is not and has never been the law of Texas. The three-offense list defines where citation is *mandatory* regardless of officer discretion; it does not define the outer boundary of permissible arrest authority.

**Second**, the R&R's constitutional analysis under *Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001), fails to account for *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008), which this Court is virtually certain to confront on de novo review. While *Moore* limits the Fourth Amendment theory, it does not defeat Plaintiff's independent § 1983 claim for unlawful arrest under Texas law — a claim that survives regardless of how the Fourth Amendment question is resolved.

**Third**, the R&R adopted the defense's Motion to Dismiss wholesale, reproduced its arguments verbatim, and failed to address — or even mention — the majority of Plaintiff's claims. This is precisely the failure that § 636(b)(1) and Rule 72(b) were designed to prevent.

## II. LEGAL STANDARD

## III. OBJECTION NO. 1: THE R&R'S STATUTORY ANALYSIS OF § 543.004(c) IS LEGALLY ERRONEOUS

### A. The R&R's "Exhaustive List" Reading Is Wrong

The R&R's central statutory holding is:

> "Whatever prohibition against arrest Thompson believes is implied by § 502.407 cannot stand in the face of the plain language of § 543.004, which articulates exactly three offenses for which officers may not make an arrest, none of which is an expired registration."

**This is a fundamental misreading of § 543.004(c).** The statute does not say: "these three and only these three offenses require citation." It says:

> "Notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter, if a person is alleged to have committed a violation of Section 545.413, 545.423, or 547.61, the person is entitled to receive a written notice to appear not later than the 20th day after the date of the alleged violation." Tex. Transp. Code § 543.004(c).

This language — "notwithstanding any other provision of this subchapter" — is a **mandatory supersession clause** applicable only to three specific offenses. It means: *even if other provisions of the Transportation Code would authorize different treatment, these three offenses require mandatory citation.* It does not mean: *because these three offenses are the only ones with mandatory citation, all other offenses permit arrest at officer discretion.*

The logical gap in the R&R's analysis is this: **the absence of an offense from § 543.004(c)'s list does not establish officer discretion to arrest.** It merely means the mandatory-citation override does not apply. Whether arrest is otherwise permitted depends on whether other statutes — such as § 502.407's specific remedial framework — limit that discretion.

### B. § 502.407 Creates a Specific Remedial Scheme That Limits Arrest Authority

Chapter 502 of the Texas Transportation Code places expired vehicle registration in **Subtitle A** (General Provisions). This is distinct from Subtitle B — "Rules of the Road" — which governs criminal traffic offenses. The legislature's choice to place registration offenses in Subtitle A reflects a deliberate classification: these are **administrative/regulatory offenses**, not criminal traffic offenses.

The remedial scheme under § 502.407 is fundamentally incompatible with custodial arrest:

- A person charged with expired registration may obtain **dismissal** by remedying the defect within **20 working days** or before the first court appearance, whichever is later. Tex. Transp. Code § 502.407(b)(1)(A).
- The maximum consequence upon failure to comply is a **$20 administrative fee**. *Id.* § 502.407(b)(2).
- There is **no provision for incarceration**, no **criminal finding of guilt**, and no **criminal record** upon compliance.

This is not a minor regulatory detail. The statutory scheme explicitly contemplates a non-custodial, non-punitive resolution. When the legislature says an offense is subject to dismissal upon compliance, carries a maximum $20 penalty, and is classified administratively in **Subtitle A** (General Provisions), the necessary implication is that **custodial arrest is not the intended enforcement mechanism.**

This is an application of the canon of statutory construction: **lex specialis derogat legi generali** — when a specific statute governs a particular subject, it controls over a general statute. *Jarecki v. G.D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961); *RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank*, 566 U.S. 639, 645 (2012). The specific remedial framework of § 502.407 operates as a limitation on the general arrest authority that might otherwise exist under § 543.003.

### C. The R&R's Atwater Analysis Is Incomplete

*Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001), is distinguishable and does not control this case. In *Atwater*, the Supreme Court addressed whether arrest for a seatbelt violation violated the **Fourth Amendment** — and held it did not, given the arrest was supported by probable cause and the offense was criminal in nature. Critically, *Atwater* addressed a **Subtitle B** offense — a criminal traffic misdemeanor — for which Texas law at that time authorized arrest.

The Texas Transportation Code has since evolved. The legislature's placement of registration offenses in **Subtitle A** (General Provisions) with a remedial scheme is post-*Atwater* legislative action that narrows the scope of offenses for which arrest is appropriate. A custodial arrest for an administrative offense governed by the Subtitle A remedial scheme of § 502.407 is a distinct category from the *Atwater* scenario, and the R&R's failure to acknowledge this distinction renders its *Atwater* analysis legally insufficient.

### D. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of the statutory question: whether § 502.407's specific remedial framework in **Subtitle A** operates as a limitation on arrest authority for expired registration, and whether Deputy Gonzales's arrest violated Texas law such that it gives rise to a § 1983 claim.

## IV. OBJECTION NO. 2: VIRGINIA V. MOORE REQUIRES DISTINGUISHING, AND PLAINTIFF'S § 1983 CLAIM IS INDEPENDENT OF FOURTH AMENDMENT

### A. The R&R's Failure to Address Virginia v. Moore Is Itself Reversible Error

The R&R's constitutional analysis relies exclusively on *Atwater*. It does not mention, let alone analyze, *Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008). This omission is significant because *Moore* is the controlling Supreme Court precedent on the precise question the R&R purports to resolve: whether an arrest that violates state law gives rise to a Fourth Amendment violation.

In *Moore*, the Supreme Court held: "We have not previously held that the Fourth Amendment is violated when police violate state law in making an arrest supported by probable cause." The Court further stated: "The Fourth Amendment does not require that probable cause be measured by state law."

This is adverse to Plaintiff's Fourth Amendment theory in one sense — but **the R&R cannot simply rely on *Atwater* without confronting *Moore*, because *Moore* is the more recent and directly applicable precedent on the state-law-arrest question.**

### B. Distinguishing Moore: The Statutory Remedial Scheme Matters

*Moore* involved an arrest for a "violation of the domestic violence protection order" — an offense for which Virginia law provided a criminal penalty. The Court held that the state law prohibition on arrest did not create a Fourth Amendment violation.

But there is a meaningful distinction: in *Moore*, Virginia law **prohibited** arrest but provided **no alternative remedial scheme**. Here, Texas law does not merely prohibit arrest for expired registration — it provides an **affirmative remedial framework** that is fundamentally incompatible with arrest:

- § 502.407 creates a right to **dismissal** upon compliance
- The maximum penalty is **$20** — not incarceration, not a criminal fine, not detention
- The remedy is administrative, not criminal

The existence of a specific statutory remedy — one that affirmatively requires dismissal and limits consequences to a nominal administrative fee — is categorically different from a mere prohibition on arrest. A reasonable officer reading § 502.407 would understand that the legislature intended this to be an administrative, non-custodial matter. This is not an argument about the Fourth Amendment — it is an argument about what a reasonable officer would understand about the scope of his statutory authority under Texas law.

### C. The § 1983 Claim Is Independent of the Fourth Amendment

Even if the Court determines that the arrest did not violate the Fourth Amendment under *Moore* and *Atwater*, Plaintiff has an **independent federal claim under § 1983** for deprivation of rights under color of state law. Section 1983 does not require a Fourth Amendment violation — it requires a deprivation of constitutional or federal statutory rights. An unlawful arrest under Texas law is actionable under § 1983 as a deprivation of the right to be free from arrest that exceeds statutory authority, regardless of whether the Fourth Amendment is implicated.

Moreover, the **due process claim** (Count II) is entirely independent of the Fourth Amendment analysis. Plaintiff's allegations regarding the denial of exculpatory footage, the procedurally defective tow hearing, and the denial of public records requests to an indigent plaintiff state independent Fourteenth Amendment claims that the R&R failed to meaningfully address.

### D. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court:
1. Conduct de novo review of whether *Virginia v. Moore* applies and, if so, whether the specific remedial scheme of § 502.407 distinguishes this case from *Moore*;
2. Confirm that Plaintiff's § 1983 claim for unlawful arrest under Texas law is independent of the Fourth Amendment analysis; and
3. Reject the R&R's recommendation that Count I be dismissed on qualified immunity grounds.

## V. OBJECTION NO. 3: THE R&R'S QUALIFIED IMMUNITY ANALYSIS IS LEGALLY ERRONEOUS

### A. The R&R Assumed Its Central Conclusion

The R&R states: "even if Thompson's theory is correct (it is not), his argument is so novel that Gonzales would be entitled to qualified immunity anyway." Dkt. 42 at 5.

This reasoning is circular and legally erroneous for three reasons.

**First**, qualified immunity protects officers only when the constitutional right at issue was not "clearly established." The question is not whether Thompson's specific theory had been previously litigated — it is whether the **constitutional right's contours** were sufficiently definite that a reasonable officer would understand he was violating it. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). The right at issue here is not a newly minted constitutional doctrine — it is the principle that an officer may not arrest for an offense that the Texas Legislature has placed in Subtitle C with a non-custodial remedial scheme and a $20 maximum penalty. A reasonable officer reading § 502.407 would understand that custodial arrest is not the intended enforcement mechanism.

**Second**, the R&R conflates two distinct questions: (1) whether a constitutional right was clearly established, and (2) whether the officer's conduct was objectively unreasonable. Once a plaintiff demonstrates a constitutional violation, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove qualified immunity. *See Thompson v. Upshur*, 245 F.3d 447, 457 (5th Cir. 2001). The R&R's analysis never reaches this burden-shifting inquiry because it assumes the conclusion that no constitutional violation occurred.

**Third**, the R&R's reliance on *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731 (2011), is misplaced. *al-Kidd* involved a dramatic expansion of executive power — the use of a federal material witness warrant as a pretextual pre-trial detention. This case involves the **narrowing** of arrest authority based on a specific statutory remedial scheme. These are opposite ends of the constitutional spectrum, and a reasonable officer reading § 502.407 would plainly understand that arrest was not the authorized remedy.

### B. The Plain Text of § 502.407 Provided Clear Notice

Critically, § 502.407 has existed in its current form for years. Its text is clear: expired registration is an administrative offense with a specific non-custodial remedy. Officers are charged with knowledge of the statutes they enforce. *See United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 266-67 (1997) (holding that "fair warning" can be provided by the plain text of a statute even without case law). If the Texas Transportation Code on its face establishes a non-custodial remedial scheme for an offense, a reasonable officer has fair warning that arrest is not the authorized response.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of the qualified immunity analysis and find that the right to be free from custodial arrest for an administrative offense governed by the Subtitle A remedial scheme of § 502.407 with a specific non-custodial remedial scheme was clearly established by the plain text of § 502.407.

## VI. OBJECTION NO. 4: THE R&R FAILED TO ADDRESS THE DUE PROCESS CLAIM REGARDING THE SHAM MAGISTRATION

### A. The R&R's Judicial Immunity Analysis Did Not Address the Due Process Claim

The R&R's analysis of Judge Sanders addresses only whether judicial immunity applies. It does not address whether the **procedures used in the magistration** — regardless of judicial immunity — violated Plaintiff's Fourteenth Amendment due process rights in a manner actionable under § 1983.

Plaintiff does not merely challenge the outcome of the magistration. He challenges the procedure itself. Judge Sanders:

1. Imposed a "time served" disposition without a formal charge being filed;
2. Accepted no plea from Plaintiff;
3. Made no finding of guilt;
4. Did not advise Plaintiff of his right to invoke the § 502.407(b) remedy;
5. Did not give Plaintiff an opportunity to cure the defect within the statutory 20-working-day period; and
6. thereby affirmatively eliminated Plaintiff's statutory right to dismissal.

This is not merely judicial error — it is a due process violation. The Due Process Clause requires, at minimum, that a person be afforded notice of the charge against them and an opportunity to be heard before a punitive disposition is entered. *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976); *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). A "time served" disposition entered without a charge, without a plea, without an adjudication, and without notice of the statutory right to cure is precisely the kind of arbitrary governmental action the Due Process Clause prohibits.

### B. The R&R Conflated Judicial Immunity with the Merits of the Due Process Claim

The R&R correctly notes that Judge Sanders is entitled to absolute judicial immunity for his adjudicative acts. But judicial immunity shields a judge from **damages liability** — it does not shield the County from **Monell liability** for maintaining a policy or custom of conducting magistrations in this manner. The R&R did not address this distinction.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of the procedural due process claim, distinguish between judicial immunity (which bars damages against Judge Sanders personally) and the merits of the due process claim (which survives against the County under Monell), and find that Plaintiff has stated a plausible due process claim.

## VII. OBJECTION NO. 5: THE R&R'S MONELL ANALYSIS IS CIRCULAR AND INCOMPLETE

### A. The R&R Assumed the Conclusion It Needed to Reach

The R&R states: "As for Matagorda County, Thompson cannot establish a constitutional violation that would give rise to Monell liability. Gonzales acted lawfully when he arrested Thompson for an expired registration; Sanders acted lawfully when he credited Thompson's time served toward the fine for an expired registration; and Chau did nothing more than appear at a tow hearing on the County's behalf."

This is circular: the Monell claim is dismissed because the underlying constitutional violations are assumed not to have occurred. But whether those violations occurred is precisely what is being contested. If this Court determines, on de novo review, that the arrest was unlawful under § 502.407, the Monell claim must be reconsidered.

### B. The Monell Claim Is Independently Strong

Even if Deputy Gonzales were entitled to qualified immunity (which Plaintiff contests), the Monell claim against Matagorda County is independently viable. Plaintiff has alleged:

1. **A policymaker with knowledge**: County officials responsible for law enforcement training and policy were aware or should have been aware that officers were making custodial arrests for administrative offenses governed by § 502.407's Subtitle A remedial scheme without following the § 502.407 remedial procedure.

2. **A policy or custom**: The County maintained a custom — evidenced by Plaintiff being subjected to the same procedure — of arresting individuals for expired registration and processing them through magistration without advising them of the § 502.407 cure right.

3. **Deliberate indifference in training**: The County failed to train officers on the distinction between Subtitle B criminal traffic offenses and Subtitle A administrative registration offenses, and failed to train JP court personnel on the procedural requirements of § 502.407.

4. **The policy was the moving force**: But for the County's policy of not advising arrestees of the § 502.407 remedy, Plaintiff would not have been subjected to 18 hours of detention and a baseless "time served" disposition.

These allegations, accepted as true, state a plausible Monell claim. *See Piotrowski v. City of Houston*, 237 F.3d 567, 578 (5th Cir. 2001). The R&R's dismissal of Monell based on a finding that no underlying constitutional violation occurred cannot stand when that finding is itself contested.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of the Monell claim, apply the correct standard for Monell liability, and find that Plaintiff has stated a plausible claim for municipal liability.

## VIII. OBJECTION NO. 6: THE R&R'S ROOKER-FELDMAN ANALYSIS IS LEGALLY INCORRECT

### A. The Rooker-Feldman Doctrine Does Not Apply

The R&R invokes Rooker-Feldman in Footnote 5 to bar Plaintiff's challenges to the state court proceedings. This is error.

The Rooker-Feldman doctrine bars federal district courts from exercising jurisdiction over "cases brought by state-court losers complaining of injuries caused by state-court judgments rendered before the district court proceedings commenced and inviting district court review and rejection of those judgments." *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Indus. Corp.*, 544 U.S. 280, 284 (2005).

Plaintiff is not asking this Court to review or reverse any state court judgment. Plaintiff's claims are independent federal claims under § 1983 that:

1. The **initial arrest** was unlawful under Texas law;
2. The subsequent **magistration and records denial** violated federal due process; and
3. Defendants **conspired** to conceal constitutional violations.

These claims do not require the Court to "review and reject" any state court judgment. They are claims about ongoing constitutional violations by government officials — precisely the claims that § 1983 was designed to address.

The Supreme Court has expressly preserved the distinction between direct appellate review of state court judgments and independent federal claims under § 1983:

> "A § 1983 action is a 'parallel' suit, not an appeal, when the plaintiff is not asking the district court to review and reverse the state court judgment, but to redress injuries caused by the state actors' unconstitutional conduct." *Argo v. Blue Cross & Blue Shield of Md.*, 339 F.3d 1089, 1096 (9th Cir. 2003) (emphasis added); *see also Dennis v. Spurs*, 875 F.3d 865, 869 (5th Cir. 2017) (Rooker-Feldman does not bar § 1983 claims that challenge defendants' conduct, not the state court judgment itself).

### B. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court reject the Rooker-Feldman argument and confirm that Plaintiff's claims are independent federal claims under § 1983, not appeals of state court proceedings.

## IX. OBJECTION NO. 7: THE R&R'S SUA SPONTE DISMISSAL OF JACKSON TOWING VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

### A. No Notice, No Opportunity to Be Heard

The R&R recommends sua sponte dismissing Plaintiff's claims against Jackson Towing Services "before the court enters final judgment" based solely on the fact that "there are only two references to Jackson in the complaint." Dkt. 42 at 7-8.

This dismissal violates due process.

1. **No motion was filed regarding Jackson**: The defense's Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 29) addressed only the Matagorda County Defendants. Jackson was not mentioned.
2. **No sua sponte show-cause order was issued**: The Court did not order Plaintiff to show cause why Jackson should not be dismissed.
3. **No opportunity to amend**: Plaintiff was not given notice that sua sponte dismissal was being considered and had no opportunity to file an amended complaint with additional factual allegations.

A sua sponte dismissal without prior notice and opportunity to amend violates the Due Process Clause. *See Alert One Co. v. Hydro-Aid, Inc.*, 299 F.3d 746, 748 (5th Cir. 2002) (a court may dismiss a claim sua sponte only after providing notice and an opportunity to amend).

### B. Plaintiff Has Stated a Plausible Claim Against Jackson

Accepting the complaint's allegations as true: Jackson Towing removed Plaintiff's vehicle from private property over the owner's objection; County Attorney Chau "permitted testimony from a Jackson Towing representative who lacked any firsthand knowledge of the towing circumstances or vehicle location"; and Jackson participated in a hearing designed to ratify an unlawful tow. These allegations are sufficient to state a plausible claim for civil conspiracy and unlawful conversion.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that any dismissal of Jackson Towing be **with leave to amend**, after appropriate notice, so that Plaintiff may allege additional factual detail to support his claims.

## X. OBJECTION NO. 8: THE R&R FAILED TO ADDRESS COUNT IV (CIVIL CONSPIRACY UNDER § 1985(3))

### A. The R&R Is Silent on Count IV

Plaintiff's Complaint and Opposition raised specific allegations of civil conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3): coordinated actions among Deputy Gonzales, Judge Sanders, County Attorney Chau, and Judge Finlay to (1) arrest Plaintiff for a non-arrestable offense; (2) eliminate Plaintiff's § 502.407 remedy through a baseless magistration; (3) conceal the circumstances of the arrest by denying footage; and (4) ratify the unlawful tow at a procedurally defective hearing.

The R&R does not contain a separate analysis of Count IV. It does not address the specific coordinated actions alleged, the overt acts, or the legal standard for § 1985(3) conspiracy liability. The only mention of § 1985(3) is in Footnote 6, in the context of prosecutorial immunity, without engaging with the substantive conspiracy allegations.

### B. The Coordination Is Sufficiently Pled

The Fifth Circuit has held that a civil conspiracy under § 1985(3) may be established by circumstantial evidence of a "meeting of the minds" among conspirators. *See Hill v. City of Pontotoc*, 983 F.3d 779, 788 (5th Cir. 2020). The sequential, coordinated pattern here — arrest for a non-arrestable offense, immediate magistration eliminating the cure right, denial of footage, and a one-sided tow hearing — is precisely the type of circumstantial evidence from which a conspiracy may be inferred.

### C. Request for Relief

Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court conduct de novo review of Count IV and address whether Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to state a claim for civil conspiracy under § 1985(3).
